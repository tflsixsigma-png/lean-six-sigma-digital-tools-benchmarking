ONE

Example 1 — The Value Case Canvas: Virginia Mason's spine clinic

The case in five facts

  • In the mid-2000s the insurer Aetna told Virginia Mason it cost more than comparable Seattle providers for the same quality, and Starbucks — an Aetna client — complained about the cost and delay of treating employees' back pain.
  • Before the redesign a patient with uncomplicated low-back pain waited about a month for an appointment, often had an MRI, and was passed between specialists before physical therapy was prescribed.
  • The redesigned pathway puts a physical therapist first, a physician alongside to separate complicated cases, and starts therapy the same day; imaging is reserved for clinical red flags, and the order-entry system blocks an MRI without indication.
  • Within a year MRI use fell from 15.4% to 10% of patients, waits fell to a day, and only 6% of patients lost time from work. Each avoided MRI saved the payer and employer about $850.
  • The clinic's own income per case went from a profit of about $100 to a loss of about $200, because it was paid for procedures it no longer performed. Virginia Mason then negotiated with Aetna to share the saving; patient volume roughly doubled.
The Value Case Canvas — filled inWritten as the project would have been appraised at approval, in 2004, by a group that knew the pathway and the payer's position
Redesign the back-pain pathway at Virginia Mason's spine clinic, Seattle (2004–2005). Sponsored by the medical center's leadership under the Virginia Mason Production System, after the payer Aetna signalled it might drop the hospital for costing more than comparable providers, and the employer Starbucks complained about the cost and the delays of back-pain treatment. Approved to achieve: faster access and lower cost per episode for uncomplicated low-back pain.

2 · The WTP edge — does the customer notice?

Delivery speedFrom a wait of about a month to a same-day appointment. Cost is moved too, but for the payer, not for the clinic.
Where a group would splitHalf the groups pick Cost. The test is who pays: the saving lands on Aetna and Starbucks, the speed lands on the patient and the employer. Both are real; the canvas asks for the one the project moves most, and speed is the one every party noticed first.
ConformanceCare that follows the evidence (physical therapy first; imaging only on red flags) instead of varying by physician. Conformance lowers cost, which is why this project pays on both edges.
Order winnerFor the payer: an order winner — Aetna had no other Seattle provider offering this pathway, so it produced preference. For the patient alone it would be a qualifier (nobody chooses a hospital for its back-pain queue, but they leave over it).
Where a group would splitThe answer depends on who the customer is — the next line. Groups that write "the patient" here will call it a qualifier; groups that write "the payer" will call it a winner. Make the group settle the customer first, then return to this line.
Three candidates: the patient, the employer (Starbucks, which loses workdays) and the payer (Aetna, which pays for MRIs). The one who decides whether the hospital keeps the business is the payer, so the canvas is written to Aetna. All three noticed: wait fell from about a month to a day; only 6% of patients lost time from work.
Where a group would splitThis is the hardest line on the canvas and the one that decides the verdict. A group that answers "the patient" writes a good clinical project; a group that answers "the payer" writes a strategic one. Neither is wrong — but they lead to different field-6 decisions.

3 · Map the stream — where the time actually goes

call for appointment → wait → physician visit → MRI ordered (for many) → wait for imaging → specialist referral → wait → physical therapy prescribed → therapy starts
Roughly two to three hours of clinical contact across the episode: a physician visit, an imaging slot, a specialist visit, a first therapy session.
About a month from first call to the start of treatment, in the pre-redesign pathway reported by the Wall Street Journal; Kenney's account of the same clinic gives waits of up to 45 days.
In two queues: the wait for the first appointment, and the loop through imaging and specialist referral before therapy — which, for about 80% of patients, changed nothing in the treatment eventually prescribed.
Where a group would splitGroups want to put the gap at "the MRI". The MRI is a step with a cost; the gap is the waiting around it. Separating the two is the point of asking for process time and lead time as different numbers.

4 · The cost edge — name the waste

Over-processingImaging and specialist consultation beyond what the clinical evidence requires for uncomplicated back pain. MRI use fell from 15.4% to 10% of patients within a year (WSJ); Kenney reports a larger fall over a longer period.
Where a group would splitThe obvious answer is Waiting, because the wait is what the patient feels. But the waiting was produced by the over-processing: remove the unnecessary imaging loop and the queue behind it disappears. The canvas asks for the waste that causes the others.
At the physician visit, where an MRI was ordered by habit rather than by indication, and in the referral to a specialist before conservative treatment had been tried.
Waiting (both queues); transport (handoffs between primary care, imaging and specialists); defects, in the sense of care that did not match the evidence.
About $850 per avoided MRI for the payer and employer; return to a same-day first appointment; patient volume at the clinic roughly doubled after the redesign.

5 · What the project actually builds

Almost nothing new. The same clinic, the same imaging equipment. One addition: an order-entry rule in the IT system that blocks an MRI order when the evidence-based indication is absent.
The whole change is here: a physical therapist sees the patient first, a physician joins to separate complicated from uncomplicated cases, therapy starts the same day, imaging is reserved for red flags.
The ability to redesign a care pathway against a payer's complaint and hold the new standard — demonstrated here, and reused later on other pathways. But note the strict test: the pathway itself is P. C is only what the organisation can now do again without this team.
Where a group would splitMost groups leave C empty for this project, and that is defensible. The case for C rests on what Virginia Mason did next with the same method, not on this project alone. Ask the group: if the people who ran this left, would the next pathway be redesigned?

6 · The verdict

A year or twoThe pathway was published; any clinic could copy the sequence. What is harder to copy is the willingness to lose money per case while the payment model catches up.
Fund with conditionsThe project destroyed the clinic's own margin: per case, roughly a $100 profit became a $200 loss, and each avoided MRI removed about $450 of the hospital's profit. It only makes sense if the payer shares the saving — which is the condition Virginia Mason then negotiated with Aetna.
Where a group would splitThis is where the customer line pays off. A group that wrote "the patient" in field 2 will vote Fund and miss the margin problem. A group that wrote "the payer" sees that the project is a value transfer and that the condition is a contract, not a process. The canvas was built to make that difference visible.
A payment arrangement that returns part of the payer's saving to the clinic; a standard that survives the physicians who designed it; and the same method applied to a second pathway within a year — the only evidence that C, rather than P, was built.
What this example teaches The canvas forced one decision the business case did not: who the customer is. Written to the patient, the project is an unambiguous improvement. Written to the payer, it is a value transfer that the clinic cannot afford unless the contract changes. Both readings are true; only the second produces a condition that can be acted on. The second lesson is quieter: every number in fields 3 and 4 was available before the project started, and nobody had put process time and lead time on the same line.

Example 2 — The Digital Tool Appraisal: Zillow Offers

The case in five facts

  • Zillow Offers, launched in 2018, bought homes directly from sellers at prices generated by Zillow's machine-learning valuation model, renovated them and resold them — "iBuying".
  • In 2021, under an internal push employees called Project Ketchup (to catch up with Opendoor), offers were adjusted upward. Zillow ended up buying about 74% of the homes it made offers on, against a target of 50–60%, and acquired roughly 15,000 homes in nine months after 4,162 in all of 2020.
  • Renovation and resale did not scale with purchases: repair budgets were squeezed by the higher offers, and contractors reported deprioritising Zillow's jobs.
  • On 2 November 2021 Zillow announced it would wind the business down, taking a write-down of about $304 million on inventory in the third quarter, a Homes-segment pre-tax loss of $422 million, a further $240–265 million of expected losses in the fourth quarter, and a 25% reduction of its workforce.
  • The CEO's explanation: "the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated."

1 · The tool

Zillow Offers' automated pricing — the Zestimate-based valuation model used to make cash offers on homes, plus the "Project Ketchup" adjustments that raised offers to win more of them (2018–2021).
Pricing and acquisition: the model estimated a home's value and its expected resale price; offers were generated from that estimate; homes were bought, renovated and resold.
In production more than a yearAppraised here as it stood in the third quarter of 2021, when the business was wound down.

2 · VCAP triage — what does it actually change?

A and PThe model was an asset, and it changed the flow of work: offers were set by the algorithm at a volume no human pricing team could have produced. It did not make the organisation better at improving its own pricing — the opposite, as the second half of 2021 showed.
Where a group would splitSome groups argue for A, P and C because Zillow had a decade of data and talent. Apply the test: what could the organisation do afterwards that it could not do before, that did not depend on the system? Nothing visible — the capability was the system.
Not claimed.
No — it only makes starting work easierNo — it only made starting work easier. Little's Law did not stop at the screen.
The one placeThe estimate replaced human pricing judgement rather than sitting beside it. That is what made the failure systematic rather than local.
Fully adopted: by late 2021 Zillow was buying about 74% of the homes it made offers on, against an internal target of 50–60%. Adoption was not the problem. It was the mechanism.
MonthsOpendoor and Offerpad ran comparable models. The model was never the advantage; the capital was.

3 · Capability maturity — the six conditions

No, only modelling or vendor materialIn 2018 no organisation had a measured result for algorithmic home-buying at scale; the evidence was the model's own back-tests.
No, or performance has driftedThe estimate was adequate for its original purpose — informing a listing — and was pushed into a different one: forecasting a resale price months ahead in a market that changed regime in 2021. The CEO's own words: "the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated."
Where a group would splitA group will say "Yes — the Zestimate was accurate". Ask: accurate at what, and for which decision? The condition is about the threshold actually in use — a cash offer — not about a median error on a website.
Yes — named recipient, inside the decision windowThe output went straight into the offer. The condition is met, and that is precisely what removed the human check.
Where a group would splitThe surprising reading. Groups expect a failed implementation to fail at integration. This one succeeded at integration and failed because nothing stood between the estimate and the money.
The offer engine, at the point of offer. Used on every offer; adjusted upward under Project Ketchup to win more of them.
No — identification without capacity to actThe action after purchase is renovation and resale, and the organisation could not act at the rate the model was buying: repairs were under-budgeted, contractors walked, homes sat.
Renovate and relist within the holding period. Owned by local operations teams; dependent on contractor capacity, repair budgets and resale demand — none of which the model saw.
Partly — indicator named, no baselineThe indicator in use was growth: homes acquired and revenue toward a $20 billion ambition. Unit margin, the indicator that would have shown the problem early, was not the one being steered.

No comparison groupThe company's own before-and-after, plus a regulatory filing and press reporting. Good enough to locate the break; not an experiment.

4 · Philosophical debt audit — AI only

Teleology — what it is told to want

Formally: an accurate resale estimate. In practice, under Project Ketchup: offers high enough to win 50–60% of them. Two objectives — accuracy and volume — with no written rule for the moment they conflicted. They conflicted in mid-2021.
Not documentedIt was implicit in the configuration: the upward adjustment was applied to the estimate without anyone writing down what it was allowed to cost in margin.
Where a group would splitThis is the field that makes the case worth teaching. Groups look for a model error. The model behaved as configured; the organisation had never stated what it wanted more — to be right or to be big.

Epistemology — what it is allowed to count as knowing

It returns an answer with no stated confidenceIt returned an answer with no stated confidence that reached the decision. A valuation model knows its own error distribution; nothing in the offer process widened the margin or escalated when the market left the training regime.
A change in the relationship between today's price and the price in three months — exactly what the 2021 market did. The data was invalidated; the system kept answering.

Ontology — what it is able to see

The thing cared about was profit per home. The stand-in was estimated resale value. Missing from the representation: renovation cost and capacity, contractor availability, holding time, and the behaviour of other iBuyers bidding on the same houses.
Only when something goes wrongOnly when something went wrong — and by then the inventory was bought.

5 · The verdict

Philosophical · Capability · Technical1 · Philosophical — no hierarchy between accuracy and volume. 2 · Capability — conditions 3 and 5: no capacity to act on what was bought, and no count of what the gain cost. 3 · Technical — the model drifted, but it was the last of the three to break.
Where a group would splitGroups rank Technical first because the CEO blamed forecasting. Push back: the forecasting error was survivable at 4,000 homes a year; it was fatal at 15,000 with the offers raised. The debt that killed the business was the one nobody had written down.
PauseThe debt has to be paid first. Appraised in mid-2021, the instrument says: stop raising offers until the objective hierarchy is written and the unit margin is the indicator being steered. The company chose "do not proceed" in November 2021, at a higher price.
Write the rule for the conflict between accuracy and volume, and put a margin floor under the adjustment. Owner: the executive who owns the Homes P&L, not the data-science team.

Benchmarking Study · 3 · Capability gaps and the six-month roadmap

High value · High feasibility · Months 0–2A written hierarchy of objectives, with a margin floor on any adjustment to the model's estimate.
High value · Medium feasibility · Months 0–2A cap on work in progress: homes under contract limited to renovation and resale capacity.
Medium value · Medium feasibility · Months 2–4Uncertainty that reaches the decision — offers widen or escalate when the model's error on recent sales exceeds a threshold.
Every offer carries a margin floor; purchases are capped at the capacity to renovate them; and the Homes P&L reads unit margin, not units.
What this example teaches The instrument does not find a model error, and that is the finding. Condition 2 was met — the estimate reached the decision — and conditions 3 and 5 were not: nobody could act on what was bought at that rate, and nobody counted what the growth cost. Underneath both sat an unwritten hierarchy of purposes. Philosophical debt accumulated where C should have been: an organisation that cannot say whether it wants to be right or to be big has no object on which to run an improvement routine. The CEO's explanation names the symptom; the appraisal locates the cause two links upstream.

THREE

What the two examples have in common

  • Both turn on a definition nobody had written down. At Virginia Mason, who the customer is. At Zillow, what the system is for when its objectives conflict. The instruments are built to force those two sentences onto paper.
  • Both locate the gap away from where the organisation was looking. The hospital looked at the MRI; the gap was the queue around it. Zillow looked at forecasting error; the gap was capacity to act and an uncounted cost.
  • Neither verdict is "fund" or "do not fund". Both are conditional — a contract in one case, a written rule and a cap in the other. That is the characteristic output of both instruments, and the reason they produce no score.

Your own project and your own tool will be less dramatic than these two, which is why they were chosen: the discipline is easier to see at full scale. Open the Value Case Canvas, the Digital Tool Appraisal and the Benchmarking Study with the Where a group would split notes in mind.
