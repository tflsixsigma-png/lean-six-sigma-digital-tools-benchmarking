The Value Case Canvas

An instrument for appraising one authorised project by what a business case usually leaves out: which edge of value it moves, which named waste it removes, and whether the organisation ends up more capable or only better equipped.

← Back to the 17 October material Versão em português

A conventional business case answers one question: does the return exceed the cost? That question is necessary and it is not sufficient, because it is silent on three things that decide whether the return survives — and the canvas asks exactly those three.

It is the group activity that closes the morning session, Lean Six Sigma & Continuous Improvement. It is worked in groups of three or four on one real project chosen from those the members bring, and it is designed so that it can be filled in without the instructor: Part One below sets out the few ideas each field relies on, Part Two is the canvas itself, and Part Three says how to read what comes out.

TWO

Filling in the canvas

The raw material One authorised project from your own organisation — in flight or recently approved — that you know well enough to defend. It does not need to be large, successful, or yours. Groups choose one project from among those their members propose, and the choice takes ninety seconds: pick the one the group disagrees about, not the one with the best business case.

Why this appraisal differs from a standard business case

  • Which edge of value does this move? A project that only lowers cost is competing on the edge where every rival is also working. A project that raises willingness to pay is harder, rarer, and usually under-argued because the number is harder to produce.
  • Is the cost saving a named waste, or an aspiration? "Improves efficiency by 15%" is a target. "Eliminates the three re-approvals caused by incomplete submissions" is a waste with a name, and only the second can be verified afterwards.
  • Does the organisation end up more capable, or only better equipped? A project that delivers assets and processes can be replicated by any competitor willing to spend the same money. A project that leaves the organisation better at improving cannot.

Those three questions are one question in disguise, and VC states it: a project is worth defending to the extent that it widens the gap between (WTP − cost) for your firm and the same quantity for the competitor you are actually losing deals to. Field 6 is where that is settled.

2 · The WTP edge — does the customer notice?

Identify whether this project raises what someone would be willing to pay, and be precise about the mechanism.

The four operational things a customer can be paying for, from Part Two. Pick the one this project moves most.
Conformance lowers cost. Reliability, durability and serviceability raise willingness to pay.
If the honest answer is that nobody outside would notice, say so. Many sound projects move only the cost edge.

3 · Map the stream — where the time actually goes

Sketch the process the project touches, at the level of three to six steps. You are not drawing a full value stream map in ten minutes; you are producing the two numbers a map exists to produce, and locating the gap between them.

Three to six, each with who performs it. Separate them with arrows: request → triage → approval → execution.
Add up the hands-on time across the steps. An estimate the group can defend is enough.
Calendar time, not working time. The ratio of the two is your flow efficiency, and it is usually between two and ten per cent.
Name the one or two places that hold most of the elapsed time. That is where the project should be aimed, and it is frequently not where it is aimed today.

4 · The cost edge — name the waste

Now name which of the wastes lives in the gap you just located. Name it from the list; do not substitute a benefit for a waste.

Optional.
Optional. Days, hours, percentage — whatever you can defend.

The test: "reduces costs" and "improves productivity" are not wastes — they are consequences. If the group cannot name a waste from the list, the project may be removing something real that nobody has yet defined, which is itself a finding worth reporting. And if the waste removed is overproduction, note it: that is the root waste, and the project is probably worth more than its business case claims.

5 · What the project actually builds

Decompose the deliverable into the three terms. Most projects touch more than one; few touch all three.

What is bought or built?
What flow changes, and how?
What will this organisation be able to do afterwards that it could not do before — and that does not depend on the new system?

The test for C is strict. Training people to operate the new system is not capability — it is part of delivering the asset. Capability means the organisation ends the project better at improving processes than it was before: a routine that survives, a standard that is now owned, a group that can now run the next improvement without external help. Most projects leave C untouched, and that is a legitimate answer.

6 · The verdict

The group presents field 1 and field 6, with the reasoning that connects them. Three minutes, one spokesperson.

THREE

How to read the result

What to expect

Two results are common enough to be worth naming in advance, and neither is a failure of the project you chose.

Only one edge moves

Most project portfolios are built almost entirely of cost-edge projects. That is not wrong, but it is a choice, and usually an unexamined one. The question it raises: what would it take for this same project to also move willingness to pay — and would that cost much more?

C is empty

Projects deliver assets and processes because those are what can be specified, scheduled and signed off. Capability resists all three. The question it raises: if A and P are purchasable by any competitor, what in this portfolio is actually building advantage?
