Digital Tool Appraisal

An instrument for deciding what a digital tool — including an AI system — actually changes in your organisation, and what it quietly leaves undone.

← Back to the 17 October material

Three questions decide whether a digital investment returns anything. Does it change what the organisation is able to do, or only what it owns? Where it contains AI: what has the system been told to want, to know, and to see? And what is the reference already doing that you are not — the routine, not the number?

This instrument asks all three. It takes about twenty minutes for one tool, and it is designed to be filled in by the people who will live with the decision rather than by the people selling it.

ONE

Why a tool can be excellent and still return nothing

The afternoon session establishes the frame. Value is not the sum of what an organisation owns and how it works — it is that sum scaled by its capacity to improve.

V = C × ( A + P )
assets and processes are additive and purchasable · capability is a coefficient

A platform is almost always an A decision, sometimes a P decision, and very rarely a C decision. That is not a criticism of platforms — it is a statement of what they are. The error is paying for A and booking the benefit as though C had moved.

Technical debt accumulates in A and P. Philosophical debt accumulates where C should be.

Schrage and Kiron argue that the binding constraint on value from AI is not technical but philosophical: organisations invest heavily in algorithms, data infrastructure and talent while never articulating what their systems should value, how they should know, and what they should represent about reality. The unarticulated assumptions do not stay harmless. They compound.

The parallel with technical debt is exact, and so is the difference. Technical debt is a shortcut in the artefact. Philosophical debt is an absence in the organisation — nobody wrote down the hierarchy of purposes, so nobody can audit it, so nobody can improve it. An organisation that cannot say what its system is optimising for has no basis on which to run an improvement routine over it. That is a deficit in C, arriving dressed as a deficit in A.

The three pillars, and where you have already met them

Each of Schrage and Kiron's pillars restates something the day has already established in another vocabulary. That is the reason to take them seriously rather than as a separate topic.

PillarWhat it governsWhere it appears in the session
TeleologyWhat the system should achieve — the hierarchy of purposes, and what happens when objectives conflictOrder winners and qualifiers. A system optimising the wrong metric is one whose purposes were never ranked — the same error as over-investing in a qualifier.
EpistemologyWhat counts as valid knowledge — how the system acquires it, validates it and expresses certaintyThe digital andon. A system that knows when it does not know, and escalates, is signalling at the source rather than reporting on a cycle.
OntologyHow the system represents reality — which entities it recognises, which relations it models, which variables it ignoresCausal ambiguity. Representing a supplier by price and lead time alone is the same error as benchmarking the indicator instead of the practice that produces it.
The failure these predict A sourcing system optimised for cost and delivery that, two years on, has systematically excluded suppliers in regions the firm's own strategy prioritised. The fault is not in the model. It is that the hierarchy of objectives was never written down, so the trade-off was resolved by default — and silently, because nothing in the architecture was built to surface it.

Why this matters more for AI than for anything else you buy

Every tool embeds assumptions. What distinguishes an AI system is that it acts on them at scale, continuously, and without re-stating them. A spreadsheet with a wrong assumption produces one wrong number that somebody reads. A model with an unarticulated teleology produces ten thousand decisions a month, each technically correct and strategically wrong, and reports high confidence throughout.

This sharpens as systems become agentic. A predictive system recommends and a human decides; an agentic system formulates plans and acts. At that point the hierarchy of purposes stops being documentation and becomes the control surface — the only place where the boundary of autonomy is actually set.

TWO

The instrument

Appraise one tool at a time. Answer from what the organisation can actually evidence, not from what the business case promised. "Not documented" and "nobody knows" are the most useful answers here — they are precisely what the instrument is looking for.

Digital Tool Appraisal About 20 minutes for one tool · the AI layer appears only if it applies

1 · The tool

Be specific. "Improves collaboration" is not a workflow.
Anything that learns from data, scores, ranks, forecasts, classifies or generates. If a vendor calls it AI and you are not sure what it does, answer yes — that uncertainty is itself a finding.

2 · VCAP triage — what does it actually change?

Training people to operate the new system is part of delivering the asset. C means the organisation ends up better at improving its processes than it was before.
Leave blank if you did not claim C. A blank here is a legitimate and common answer.
A tool that makes work easier to start without capping what is in progress mechanically lengthens lead time.
Of the people meant to use it as the working record, what share actually do? Write "too early" if it is not live.
The philosophical layer below applies to tools that contain AI or machine learning. Answer question 1.4 above and it will appear if it is relevant.

3 · Philosophical debt audit

Three pillars, after Schrage and Kiron. The question in each case is not whether the system is good, but whether the organisation has articulated the assumption — because an assumption nobody wrote down is one nobody can audit or improve.

Teleology — what it is told to want

The hierarchy of purposes. Most critical, most often skipped.
The actual objective function or target metric, not the business goal it was sold against.
When cost, service level, risk and sustainability conflict, is it written down which wins?
Every optimiser trades something away. If you cannot name what, that is the finding.

Epistemology — what it is allowed to count as knowing

How it acquires knowledge, validates it, and expresses certainty.
A model learns the conditions that generated its data. Name the event that would end those conditions — a market shift, a regulation, a reorganisation, a pandemic.
Does anyone in the organisation know which of the patterns it acts on are causal?

Ontology — what it is able to see

Which entities it recognises, which relations it models, which variables do not exist for it.
Geopolitical exposure, a relationship, a regulatory change, a supplier's own fragility. If nothing comes to mind in sixty seconds, that is worth noting as an answer.

4 · The reference

Somebody already does this well. The question is not what their number is, but what routine produces it — and what you would have to accumulate before that routine would work here.

The most useful reference is often outside your industry. Xerox fixed its warehouse by studying a catalogue retailer, not a competitor.
The practice, not the indicator. "They are 30% faster" is a number; "they review the representation quarterly with a named owner" is a practice.
The absorption condition. Transfer fails for knowledge reasons far more often than motivational ones — including between two units of the same company.

THREE

Reading the result

The instrument has no score, deliberately. It produces three things worth looking at.

  • Which term you could defend. Most honest appraisals land on A, or on A and P. That is not a failure — it is a correctly identified purchase. The failure is claiming C and being unable to say, in one sentence, what the organisation will be able to do afterwards without the system.
  • How many pillars are unarticulated. Not how good the system is — whether anyone wrote the assumption down. A pillar nobody has articulated cannot be audited, and therefore cannot be improved. Three unarticulated pillars in a system that is already acting autonomously is the condition under which the sourcing failure above becomes likely rather than unlucky.
  • Whether the debt is payable before or only after. Teleology is cheap to articulate before deployment and expensive afterwards, because by then the system has been acting on the implicit hierarchy and the organisation has accumulated two years of decisions made under it.
The one question to take into the next steering committee Technical safeguards — value limits, audit logs, anomaly alerts — are necessary and not sufficient. Before granting any system autonomy, ask the three philosophical ones: is the hierarchy of purposes documented, including what happens when objectives conflict? Does it know when it does not know, and escalate? Can it recognise a situation it has never seen, or does it only optimise inside the world it was trained on? Without those three, autonomy is risk wearing the costume of efficiency.
