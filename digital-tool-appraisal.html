Digital Tool Appraisal

An instrument for deciding what a digital tool — including an AI system — actually changes in your organisation, what it quietly leaves undone, and which capability gaps to close first.

← Back to the 17 October material

Three questions decide whether a digital investment returns anything. Does it change what the organisation owns, or what it is able to do? At which link does the chain from a working tool to an organisational result break — or go unmeasured? And, where the tool contains AI, what has the system been told to want, to know and to see?

The instrument asks all three in the room, in about twenty minutes for one tool, and then turns outward in two follow-up sections: what a reference organisation does that you do not, and which capability gaps to close first over six months. It is designed to be filled in by the people who will live with the decision rather than by the people selling it, and "not documented" and "nobody knows" are its most useful answers.

ONE

What you need in order to answer

Everything below is developed in the afternoon's reading notes. This page carries only the three ideas the fields depend on.

A tool enters the bracket; it does not touch the coefficient

V = C × ( A + P )
assets and processes are additive and purchasable · capability is a coefficient

A platform is almost always an A decision — the organisation owns something new. Sometimes it is also a P decision — the way work flows changes. It is very rarely a C decision, which would mean the organisation ends up better at improving its processes than it was before. The error that destroys business cases is paying for A and booking the benefit as though C had moved.

Six conditions between a working tool and a result

0Evidence basethe tool
1Technical capability in operationthe tool
2Workflow integrationcapability 1
3Translation into actioncapability 2
4Organisational outcomethe result
5Net valuethe result

Each is necessary and none is sufficient: a break at any link stops the effect, however good the links before it. The two in the middle are not properties of the tool at all — they are capabilities of the organisation that bought it, and they are where a systematic review of real implementations found value most often lost. The chain is drawn from that review (Lima, manuscript in preparation); its evidence base is hospital implementations and it is used here as a structure for appraisal, not as a citable result.

Where the tool contains AI: three things nobody wrote down

PillarThe questionWhat it looks like when it is missing

The three questions are Schrage and Kiron's (Philosophy Eats AI, MIT Sloan Management Review, 2025); "philosophical debt", the name this course gives to what accumulates when they go unasked, is from Excelência Operacional, Chapter 5. An assumption nobody wrote down is one nobody can audit or improve — which is why section 4 asks not whether the system is good, but whether the assumption was articulated.

TWO

The instrument

Appraise one tool at a time. Answer from what the organisation can actually evidence, not from what the business case promised. "Not documented" and "nobody knows" are the most useful answers here — they are precisely what the instrument is looking for.

Digital Tool Appraisal In the room: sections 1 to 5, about twenty minutes for one tool. Sections 6 and 7 are the follow-up assignment.

1 · The tool

Be specific. "Improves collaboration" is not a workflow.
Anything that learns from data, scores, ranks, forecasts, classifies or generates. If a vendor calls it AI and you are not sure what it does, answer yes — that uncertainty is itself a finding.

2 · VCAP triage — what does it actually change?

Training people to operate the new system is part of delivering the asset. C means the organisation ends up better at improving its processes than it was before.
Leave blank if you did not claim C. A blank here is a legitimate and common answer.
Work in progress is everything started and not yet finished. With the same capacity, more in flight means each item waits longer — Little's Law from the morning. A tool that makes work easier to start without capping what is in progress lengthens lead time by construction.
Take the main thing the tool records: a schedule date, a risk, a cost, a customer. Afterwards, is there a single place to look for it, with every other system reading from there? If the same information also keeps living in a spreadsheet or in the old system, somebody reconciles the copies forever, and the dashboards become negotiations.
Of the people meant to do their work inside it — not just log in — what share actually do? Write "too early" if it is not live. A tool used by 40% of its intended users does not deliver 40% of a single-source benefit; it delivers close to none, because the other 60% still has to be reconciled.
This is the test for whether the tool buys advantage or only parity. Anything purchasable is available to a rival at roughly the same price.

3 · Capability maturity — the six conditions

The chain from a working tool to an organisational result, after Lima (manuscript in preparation). It applies to any tool; where the tool contains AI, conditions 0 and 1 bite harder. Each condition is necessary and none is sufficient, so the useful answer is the first one that is not a clear yes. Not measured is a legitimate and common answer — and a more tractable finding than a failure.

Read across, the six answers are a maturity statement, and a more defensible one than a stage number: they say which conditions this organisation can currently satisfy and demonstrate, which it satisfies without evidence, and which it has never observed. There is no composite score here on purpose. A single figure would hide the only thing worth knowing, which is where the chain stops.

"Kind of application" means the job: demand forecasting, scheduling, document generation, risk scoring, case prioritisation. Not a model, not a simulation, not a pilot that reported accuracy — an implementation somebody measured afterwards, ideally in an organisation like yours.
For any tool: has anyone checked, after go-live, that it performs as specified on our volumes and our exceptions? For an AI tool, more sharply: vendor accuracy was measured on the vendor's data with the vendor's settings — has it been checked on ours, with the cut-off we actually use, counting only outputs that tell people something they did not already know?
The first of the two organisational conditions. Name the person and the step, not the dashboard.
Two thirds of staff rarely or never opening the tool is the commonest observed failure, and it is invisible unless somebody asks.
The second organisational condition. If acting on the output needs three approvals, a budget line and a resource nobody has, an earlier warning changes nothing — the constraint was never information.
Write the sentence the business case should contain: when the output says X, who does what, using what.
One named indicator, one data source, one baseline — and not a satisfaction survey. Where perception and objective measurement were collected together, they diverged.
Who is de-prioritised, which work moves to other people or other hours, and what checking the output takes. The hour saved in the back office that reappears as an hour of overtime in the front office is not a gain; it is a relocation.
Three things make a result trustworthy: a comparison group measured at the same time, an objective measure rather than what people said they felt, and a source that is not selling the tool. Tick the weakest point in what you relied on. Vendor material usually fails at least two of the three.
Section 4 applies only to tools that contain AI or machine learning. Answer the last question of section 1 — Does it include AI or machine learning? — and it will appear if it is relevant.

4 · Philosophical debt audit — AI only

Three pillars, after Schrage and Kiron. The question in each case is not whether the system is good, but whether the organisation has articulated the assumption — because an assumption nobody wrote down is one nobody can audit or improve.

Teleology — what it is told to want

The hierarchy of purposes. Most critical, most often skipped.
The number the system actually tries to maximise or minimise — cost per order, forecast error, time to resolution — not the business goal it was sold against. If nobody can say, write "unknown". That is the finding, not a gap in your answer.
When cost, service level, risk and sustainability conflict, is it written down which wins?

Epistemology — what it is allowed to count as knowing

How it acquires knowledge, validates it, and expresses certainty.
A model learns the conditions that generated its data. Name the event that would end those conditions — a market shift, a regulation, a reorganisation, a pandemic.

Ontology — what it is able to see

Which entities it recognises, which relations it models, which variables do not exist for it.
Schrage and Kiron's example: a company wants loyal customers and hands the system recency, frequency, spend and NPS. Those are proxies, not loyalty, and the system optimises the proxies. Write the thing you care about, the measure the system was given instead, and one thing that matters to the decision which that measure cannot see — a relationship, a regulatory change, a supplier's own fragility. The map is not the territory.

5 · The verdict

More than one is usually present. Put the one that would do most damage if left alone at the top; leave the lower ranks blank if only one or two apply. Technical is a shortcut in the artefact itself. Philosophical is purpose, knowledge or representation that nobody wrote down. Adoption is a tool that works and is not used. Capability is a tool the organisation could not improve after buying it.
After the session — the benchmarking exercise and the roadmap Section 6 is the benchmarking exercise: one organisation or standard that already does this well, and what it would take to do it here. Section 7 is the six-month roadmap across the organisation's three largest capability gaps. Together they are the module's follow-up assignment, and they are better done with a week of thought than with ten minutes of room time.

6 · Benchmarking — who already does this well, and what it would take to do it here

This is the benchmarking exercise. Pick one organisation, or one published standard, that does what this tool is meant to achieve better than you do. The question is not what their number is, but what routine produces it — and what you would have to build before that routine would work here.

The most useful reference is often outside your industry. Xerox fixed its warehouse by studying a catalogue retailer, not a competitor.
The practice, not the indicator. "They are 30% faster" is a number; "they review the representation quarterly with a named owner" is a practice.
What would our people need to already know, or already be able to do, for this to take here? Practices fail to transfer for reasons of knowledge far more often than of motivation — including between two units of the same company.

7 · Capability gaps and the six-month roadmap

The appraisal above concerns one tool. A roadmap concerns the organisation. Name the three capabilities whose absence most constrains performance — capabilities, not products: "a single authoritative home for schedule dates" rather than "a new PPM system" — then place each one on the two axes that decide sequence. For each gap, answer three questions. How much does closing it matter? High if it moves something customers choose you for, or removes a constraint that keeps recurring; low if it is tidier but changes no result. Can it be closed with what exists today? High if the people, the data and the authority to change the process are already in hand; low if one of the three has to be built first. When would work on it start? — not finish: a roadmap is sequenced by starts.

One sentence, measurable. A roadmap whose first checkpoint cannot be stated is a list of intentions with dates attached.

THREE

Reading the result

The instrument has no score, deliberately. It produces three things worth looking at.

  • Which term you could defend. Most honest appraisals land on A, or on A and P. That is a correctly identified purchase, not a failure. The failure is claiming C and being unable to say, in one sentence, what the organisation will be able to do afterwards without the system.
  • Where the chain first stops being a clear yes. That link is the finding. If it is condition 2 or 3, the problem is the organisation's and no vendor can fix it. If the answer is "not measured", the next step is to measure, not to spend — an unobserved implementation is a far more tractable problem than a failing one.
  • Where the tool contains AI, how many pillars nobody wrote down. Not how good the system is — whether the assumption was articulated. Teleology is cheap to write down before deployment and expensive afterwards, once the system has been acting on the implicit hierarchy for two years.
